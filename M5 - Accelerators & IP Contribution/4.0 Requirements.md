### From business requirements to functional and infrastructure requirements

### 1. Problem statement

High-level business goals (e.g., "help customer support agents answer queries faster") are vague, uncheckable targets that cannot directly dictate platform architecture or system verification. Choosing deployment infrastructure or AI model delivery channels based on developer preference rather than formal, derived constraints leads to indefensible platform choices, regulatory non-compliance, unsupportable latency or scale bottlenecks, and failed quality reviews.

### 2. Summary

Translating business problems into production deployment architectures requires establishing checkable functional requirements and deriving infrastructure constraints before selecting a deployment platform:

* **Functional Requirements**: Unambiguous, testable behavioral statements defining exactly *what* the AI system must do (e.g., "classify each ticket into one of four queues," "draft a reply citing the relevant policy," "never auto-send without human approval"). These checkable statements serve directly as evaluation lines in automated test suites and review criteria.
* **Infrastructure Requirements**: Non-functional constraints derived by interrogating the business context across four critical operational dimensions:
  * **Latency**: End-to-end response time targets measured from the end-user location.
  * **Scale**: Expected request volume and peak concurrency throughput.
  * **Residency**: Geographical boundaries and regulatory frameworks governing data processing and storage.
  * **Identity & Auditability**: Authorization boundaries, credential models, user/agent identity tracking, and audit logging standards.


* **Requirements Record & Defensibility**: Documenting functional behaviors alongside infrastructure constraints and their governing regulations produces a defensible artifact. This record ensures platform decisions (e.g., self-hosted vs. enterprise cloud gateway vs. managed API) follow logically from requirements rather than developer familiarity.
* **Trade-offs & Scope Applicability**: Deriving infrastructure constraints up front requires a scoping conversation teams may be tempted to skip. While essential for production and regulated systems, throwaway prototypes without regulatory constraints or formal reviews can rely on lightweight notes instead.

### 2. Clear & Simple Explanation

Imagine you are hired to build a house. If the client tells you, "I want a comfortable home," that is a vague business goal—you cannot buy materials or pick a foundation based on the word "comfortable."

To turn that goal into an actual engineering plan, you break it down into two types of requirements:

1. **What the house must do (Functional Requirements)**: "It must have 3 bedrooms, auto-locking exterior doors, and a fire sprinkler system." These are clear pass/fail features that inspectors can test.
2. **The physics and rules of the building site (Infrastructure Requirements)**: "How heavy is the snow load in winter? What is the water pressure? What are local zoning laws?" (Latency, Scale, Data Residency, and Identity).

If you pick the foundation or building materials before knowing the local climate and zoning regulations, the building may fail inspections or collapse under load. Writing down these requirements first lets you prove to building inspectors (system reviewers) why you selected a specific foundation (deployment platform).

### 4. Real-World Application

**Scenario: Enterprise Healthcare Claims Processing Assistant**

A health insurance provider aims to automate medical claims processing ("reduce turnaround time for patient claims"). Before selecting a deployment platform (e.g., direct public API vs. AWS Bedrock VPC vs. self-hosted model), the engineering team creates a formal **Requirements Record**:

* **Functional Requirements**: System must extract ICD-10 codes from incoming claim PDFs, cross-reference policy guidelines, generate approval/denial rationale citing exact policy clauses, and route edge cases to human adjusters.
* **Infrastructure Requirements**:
  * *Residency & Regulation*: HIPAA compliance requiring Zero Data Retention (ZDR) and processing restricted to US-East region.
  * *Latency*: Sub-3-second P95 target for interactive adjuster UI.
  * *Scale*: Support peak throughput of 500 concurrent claim evaluations.
  * *Identity & Auditability*: Model tool calls execute under delegated adjuster OAuth credentials with full context audit logging.

### 5. Key Terms Note Section

Preserving technical accuracy is critical when preparing for the Claude Certified Developer – Foundations exam.

**Key Technical Terms**

* **Functional Requirement**: A clear, checkable statement of system behavior specifying exact actions an AI system must perform, which directly translates into unit evaluation criteria and review benchmarks.
* **Infrastructure Requirement**: A non-functional constraint—encompassing latency, scale, data residency, and identity—governing deployment platform selection and system runtime boundaries.
* **Data Residency**: The legal and regulatory requirement mandating where customer data and AI inference processing must geographically reside and operate.
* **Identity Model**: The authorization framework defining who or what persona the agent acts under, which credentials it uses for tool invocations, and what security events are logged for compliance.
* **Requirements Record**: A formalized, documented artifact linking functional capabilities and infrastructure constraints to underlying business goals and regulations to defend platform architecture decisions during reviews.
* **Defensible Deployment Choice**: An architectural selection of deployment platform (e.g., cloud provider, managed API, direct SDK) directly justified by documented functional and non-functional infrastructure requirements rather than developer preference.

### 6. Exam Practice

--- **Exam Practice** ---

1. A developer is building an automated customer service agent for a regulated financial institution. The business sponsor states: "The agent should help users update their billing address faster." Which statement correctly represents a properly derived **functional requirement** according to architectural best practices?

* A) The deployment platform must support P99 inference latencies below 500ms under a peak load of 2,000 requests per second.
* B) The system must process all context tokens within a SOC 2 Type II compliant US-based data center.
* C) The agent must parse address inputs, validate them against the postal database, and queue changes for human authorization before updating records.
* D) The system architecture must utilize an enterprise deployment gateway supporting OAuth2 identity context propagation.

---

2. During an architectural review, an enterprise architect asks why a engineering team selected a private cloud AI gateway deployment over a public managed API. Which artifact should the developer present to defend this platform decision?

* A) A prototype execution log demonstrating high LLM output accuracy on 10 benchmark test cases.
* B) A requirements record documenting derived non-functional constraints for data residency, identity auditability, scale, and regulatory compliance.
* C) A token-cost comparison matrix estimating monthly pricing across various Claude model sizes.
* D) A prompt engineering specification detailing few-shot exemplars and system instructions.

---

3. How do properly formulated **functional requirements** directly impact the evaluation and testing lifecycle of a Claude-powered application?

* A) They configure network security groups and ingress firewall rules on the hosting platform.
* B) They specify the hardware memory configuration required to host open-source model weights.
* C) They eliminate the necessity for human-in-the-loop review mechanisms in high-risk automated workflows.
* D) They state system behavior with enough detail to serve directly as evaluation criteria and test lines in automated eval suites.

---

4. In which scenario is it acceptable for an engineering team to skip a formal upfront infrastructure scoping conversation and rely on lightweight notes instead?

* A) A throwaway internal proof-of-concept prototype operating on non-sensitive public data without formal architectural review.
* B) A multi-region customer portal dealing with European Union citizen data subject to GDPR data residency rules.
* C) An enterprise internal document search assistant requiring identity propagation and RBAC filtering.
* D) An automated medical billing agent integrating with third-party EHR APIs under HIPAA compliance.

---

--- **Answer Key & Explanations** ---

1. C - A functional requirement names what the system must do as a checkable statement of behavior (e.g., parsing address inputs, validating against postal database, requiring human authorization). Options A, B, and D describe infrastructure requirements (latency/scale, residency, and identity model).
2. B - Platform selection should be defended using a documented requirements record that links infrastructure constraints (residency, scale, identity, regulations) to the business problem. Options A, C, and D do not provide the non-functional governance and infrastructure criteria needed to defend deployment platform architecture.
3. D - Functional requirements are written as specific, checkable statements of behavior so that each functional rule can directly become an evaluation line in an eval suite and a concrete criterion during system review. Options A, B, and C are incorrect generalizations.
4. A - According to deployment decision guidelines, for a throwaway prototype with no formal review and no regulated data, lightweight notes are sufficient and skipping detailed upfront scoping is acceptable. Options B, C, and D involve regulated data, identity controls, or formal enterprise architectures that mandate documented infrastructure requirements.
